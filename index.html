Why the same machines carry more work every year

A reserved fleet behaves unlike most capital equipment. Its productive capacity rises each year while the cost basis stays fixed, so unit cost deflates. Part of that gain is exogenous. Open-weight releases arrive from outside your organisation and raise output per unit of hardware at no cost to you, the way an economy-wide productivity shock does: Qwen3 Coder Next reaches 74.2% while activating three billion parameters, so one chip now carries work that recently took several. Part is process improvement on the same capital, where a small model drafts and the large one verifies in a single pass, lowering the marginal cost of every answer. The rest is learning by doing: each month of production reveals repeating patterns, each pattern becomes a trained specialist, and your own accumulated output lowers your own future cost. What follows is ordinary operating leverage. A fixed fee spread across a rising output ceiling means average cost per million tokens falls.

Basis of estimate, sources and definitions

What each case runs. Low — the smallest models available (Ministral 3B 70%, Ministral 8B 20%, Llama 4 Scout 10%), memory held on self-managed storage, 5% of payload sampled to logging; approx. 10% accuracy, 3.7s latency. Base — a middle mix (Ministral 8B 50%, Llama 4 Scout 25%, Qwen3 Next 80B 15%, imported SLM 10%) on the managed memory service, 5% sampled; approx. 22% accuracy, 5.0s. High — GPT-5.6 Luna at $0.20/$1.20 per million tokens, asked once, full payload capture; 80% accuracy, 3.2s. Max — the same model asked three times per task with a fourth pass reading all three candidates and picking the winner, which multiplies tokens by 3.35 and raises accuracy by roughly 8 points to 88%, at 6.5s because the three samples run in parallel and only the verifier is serial. High and Max therefore differ in method rather than in model, and the same is true of Akka: 80.2% asking once, 88% asking three times. OpenAI cut Luna 80% from $1.00/$6.00 on 30 July 2026, AWS passed it through on Bedrock the same day and Microsoft matched it at parity on Azure from 1 August. Google Vertex carries no OpenAI proprietary models, so both tiers there run its strongest open weight instead, reaching 73% asked once and 81% asked three times. OpenAI published no SWE-bench Verified score for the GPT-5.6 family; published figures are SWE-bench Pro 62.7%, Terminal-Bench 2.1 84.7% and a Coding Agent Index of 75. On SWE-bench Pro, Luna leads DeepSeek V4 Pro at 55.4%, and V4 Pro scores 80.6% on Verified, so the 80% single-pass placement is reasoned rather than measured. Luna runs on AWS-managed infrastructure with in-region processing and the model provider has no access to prompts or completions, so its data-residency posture matches an open-weight model on the same platform; what a proprietary model cannot do is carry a trained adapter, run on capacity you reserve, or get cheaper as your own traffic accumulates.

Akka — MiniMax M2.5 and Qwen3 Coder Next with customer-trained LoRA adapters, served in a single pass on reserved capacity; 80.2% accuracy, 4.5s. Best-of-three sampling is not applied, so the Akka figure is the published score of the base model rather than a modelled uplift.

GCP basis. Vertex AI does not sell open-weight models by the token. Reserved capacity. Sized as continuous demand × 2.8 for peak, plus one spare node, and never below the two nodes required to hold MiniMax M2.5 and Qwen3 Coder Next resident with high availability across two availability zones. Two fleets are sized against the same work and the cheaper is taken: p5.48xlarge carrying eight NVIDIA H100 at 80GB, and p6-b200.48xlarge carrying eight NVIDIA B200 at 180GB and modelled at three times the H100 serving throughput. Below roughly 20 trillion tokens a year the H100 fleet wins, because node count is set by peak headroom rather than by throughput and the cheaper node carries the idle hours. Above that the B200 throughput advantage overtakes its higher hourly rate. Per-GPU serving throughput modelled at 25,000 tokens per second prefill and 3,000 tokens per second decode; prefill is compute-bound and decode is bound by memory bandwidth. The 2.8 peak-to-average factor is the measured figure for coding workloads across 44 million requests over nine days in the published Microsoft Azure LLM inference traces; conversational workloads in the same traces measure 1.7. Supporting compute and storage scale with deployed agents and end-user traffic at 12.5% of reserved capacity, subject to a $50,000 annual minimum. Adapter training executes against idle reserved capacity and carries no incremental charge; a trained workload consumes approximately 40% of prior input tokens and 60% of prior output tokens on a model requiring half the compute per token, and adapters are served as LoRA weights on a shared base model, adding under 1 GB of GPU memory each.

Scope. One use case carries the entire volume entered above. Inference and specialist training are both directed at that use case. Amounts are annual.

Base model. Each API model is matched to a base model. Where the API model is open weight, the base model is that same model, self-hosted. Where it is proprietary, the base model is the nearest open weight by capability class: Claude Fable 5 and Claude Cowork take Kimi K3 at 2.8 trillion total and 104 billion active parameters, the largest open-weight model; Claude Opus 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol take DeepSeek V4-Pro at 1.6 trillion total and 49 billion active; Claude Sonnet 5 takes Kimi K2.6 at 1 trillion total and 32 billion active; GPT-5.6 Terra takes GLM-5.2 at 744 billion total and 40 billion active; GPT-5.6 Luna takes MiniMax M2.5; Claude Haiku 4.5 takes Qwen3 Coder Next.

Parameter counts. Throughput scales on active parameters rather than total, since a mixture-of-experts model activates a subset of its weights per token. Qwen3 Coder Next activates three billion parameters. Kimi K3, DeepSeek V4-Pro, Kimi K2.6 and GLM-5.2 carry the active counts stated above. Ministral 3B, Ministral 8B, Phi-4 and Mistral Large 3 are dense, so active and total are the same.

Accuracy. A trained specialist model reaches approximately 90% on the task it is trained for. That figure applies where the use case is narrow. Breadth of the use case, rather than its subject, determines whether it is reached.

Training. With training off, the base model is self-hosted and served as published, and the comparison is the cost of running that model against the cost of calling an API. With training on, a specialist model is trained on the use case: it consumes 40% of the prior input tokens and produces 60% of the prior output tokens, on a model requiring half the compute per token. Prompt caching and speculative decoding apply in both cases. Off-peak batching applies in neither.

Token split by use case. Writing software 90% input, answering customers 80%, reading documents 95%, search and summary 92%, tidying data 85%. The API rate applied is the blend of that provider's input and output rates at this split, not its headline rate.

Fleet sizing. Continuous demand multiplied by 2.8 for peak, plus one spare node, and never below two nodes. The 2.8 peak-to-average factor is the measured figure for coding workloads across 44 million requests in the published Microsoft Azure LLM inference traces. Hyperscalers bill whole nodes of eight GPUs; neoclouds bill per GPU. The compute is the one selected in the provider list and the capacity picker above.

Akka fee. The Akka figure is the fee, which carries margin. Margin is 50% at or below five trillion tokens a year and declines on a logarithmic curve to 18.8% at one thousand trillion. Compute and storage follow a declining schedule per five trillion tokens: $100,000 for the first block, decaying toward a $15,000 floor, subject to a $50,000 annual minimum. Network and licence costs add $12,500 a year, plus $35,000 a year for private connectivity where Akka runs outside the customer's own cloud.

Azure AI Foundry rates verified 12 August 2026 from an aggregator; Azure renders its pricing pages client-side. Serving throughput. 25,000 tokens per second prefill and 3,000 tokens per second decode per H100 at a 12B dense equivalent, both scaling as 1/N with active parameter count, derived from floating-point throughput and memory bandwidth. Prompt caching. The input side of every per-token rate is charged at 12.07% of list. That figure is measured across 969 Claude Code transcripts and 50,279 API calls: 98.2% of context tokens were served from cache, 1.8% written to it, and a negligible share billed fresh, with cache reads billing at a tenth of the input rate and writes at 1.25 times it. Output is charged in full. Batch processing at 50% and committed-spend discounts, reported to average 28% on negotiated enterprise agreements, are available on several of these providers and are not applied. Per-outcome products bill a completed unit of work, so their price does not move with how cacheable a workload is and no cache discount is applied to them.

Per-outcome rates. Some products price a completed unit of work rather than a token. Each is converted to a per-million-token equivalent by dividing its rate by the tokens that unit consumes. A resolution or conversation is converted at 50,000 tokens, an action or generative answer at 5,000, and a Cowork task at 985,000 — the median session measured across 969 Claude Code transcripts and 50,279 API calls, at 981,000 context tokens and 4,000 output tokens, running 349 input tokens to every output token with 98.2% of context served from cache.
